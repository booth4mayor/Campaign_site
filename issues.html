The platform

Where I Stand

Elmira has the second-highest city tax rate outside New York City. What are you getting for it? We cannot expect to attract long-term residents and investment when we punish those living here with some of the highest taxes in the country. Our city's business model needs a complete overhaul, and our residents need a leader who can do it.

From day one, my administration’s priority will be to strengthen the City’s financial position and put us on a path toward lowering property taxes. We cannot expect to revitalize our local economy or attract long-term investment while facing an unstable fiscal outlook and a high tax burden. By improving financial management, controlling spending, and building a more sustainable budget, we can work toward meaningful property tax relief while ensuring the City continues to provide the essential services residents depend on.

01

Stop punishing the people who live here

Elmira has lost nearly half its population from its peak in the 1950s.1 As those people left, and our industry with them, the tax base that our city relied on failed to adapt. The burden shifted onto fewer people, driving up tax rates. An Elmira homeowner pays about 4% of their home’s assessed value in property tax every year — $39.64 per $1,000 of assessed value, city and county and school combined. The city line alone, at $18.49 per $1,000 of assessed value,1 is the second-highest city tax rate outside New York City.

That is not the only problem. A shrinking share of the county’s sales tax, and a roll where nearly 40% of the value pays nothing. Here are the numbers.

County sales tax

Of the 8% we pay at the register, the county collects half, then allocates a portion back to the city and the other municipalities within Chemung. In 2014, the city of Elmira took 12.33% of the countywide pool.2 But in 2024, it only received 8.167%. The pool grew. Our share of it shrank. Since 2015, that adds up to tens of millions we did not collect.2

The trade was supposed to bring shared services, health insurance, and county assistance. Other counties in the state have been able to make it work. The city is paying for many of those things again, but the sales tax share never came back. Our expenses are the highest in our history and our share of the county’s collections is the lowest.

What I’ll do

  • Reopen the sales tax allocation with the County.
  • Renegotiate service contracts.
  • Cut expenses where we can.

Exempt properties

Over $350 million of property value, 38.9% of the roll, is exempt and does not pay into city taxes.3 Every third building you drive past isn’t carrying its share.

  • Elmira College holds $34.8 million of assessed value across 58 city parcels. It pays the city $5,000 a year, under a 1994 student-assessment agreement priced at $5.00 per full-time resident student with a $5,000 floor. The floor holds, because the formula now produces less than the floor.4
  • Arnot Health shelters roughly $40 million and pays a little over $17,000 a year through a PILOT.4
  • The Chemung County IDA shelters over $100 million of assessed value across the city through agreements and ownership — about $3.2 million a year in taxes not collected.

The IDA’s own standard schedule is a 50% abatement over 10 years for commercial and 15 for manufacturing. The exceptions run well past it.

What I’ll do

  • Renegotiate the Elmira College agreement. It’s a 1994 contract with a floor that hasn’t moved in thirty years while the city’s costs tripled.
  • Publish an annual exemption report. Every exempt parcel, its assessed value, what it pays, and the estimated cost of the services it receives.
  • Oppose any new city abatement that exceeds the IDA’s own standard schedule.
  • Demand a formal city role at the IDA table on any deal involving city parcels.

02

Let’s start building again

Tearing a building down is half the job. The other half is what goes back on the lot.

A common saying in my family is that Elmira’s leadership is "Stuck in 1972". Many of the decisions, or lack thereof, do not address the root problems that we face, and they act like they are living in a pre-Agnes city. A majority of our businesses and storefronts from that time are gone, bulldozed and replaced with a patch of grass we call a park. A vacant lot pays nothing in tax and costs the city to mow and maintain. In Elmira the median vacant parcel carries about $20,000 of assessed value per acre. A single-family home: about $300,000 per acre. Downtown compact rows: over $1 million per acre.1

Put city-owned land back to work

The City of Elmira owns more than 200 parcels inside city limits: 28 parks, 7 parking lots, and 158 vacant lots, with a combined market value over $34 million.4 Many of those vacant lots are riverbank strips and leftover scraps of land that will never be anything else. But 37 of them are real, buildable lots worth about $1.3 million together. Right now they sit off the tax rolls, and the City pays to mow and maintain them.

The City also owns Mark Twain Golf Course, outside city limits in the Town of Horseheads. It’s supposed to pay for itself, but it lost about $160,000 in 2025 and is on pace to do the same or worse in 2026. City taxpayers cover the difference.

Parks and public safety buildings should stay public. Buildable lots and a money-losing golf course in another town should not.

Cut the red tape

And when someone does want to build, the code frequently says no. Elmira’s zoning changed in 1998.5 Since then, it has been illegal to build the homes our neighborhoods were built on — the doubles, the upstairs apartments, the shop with apartments above. A new two-family home needs a 10,000 square foot lot.5 Most city lots are about half that.

Of the city’s 8,286 residentially-zoned lots, 85% can legally hold exactly one home. Outside downtown, it’s 94%.6 Anything else means variances, hearings before the zoning boards, and months of waiting, and most people give up before they start. The last real mixed-use building we put up — 100 E. Water Street, commercial below and apartments above — went in nearly a decade ago and became the anchor everything else downtown grew around.

We already know the fix works. In 2020, the City adopted a new hybrid code for downtown as part of the Downtown Revitalization Initiative.7 Inside that area, almost every lot can hold more than one home.6 Our neighborhoods deserve the same. Elmira doesn’t need to reinvent itself. We need to let ourselves build the city we already were.

What’s left after a demolition

Then there’s First Baptist. It burned in February 2025. The city declared it a total loss and demolished it days later for public safety.2 Since February 2025, the rubble is still sitting there next to Wisner Park, in the middle of the district we tell people is coming back, and no cleanup plan has been published.3

What I’ll do

  • A demolition-to-disposition rule. No city-funded demolition without a written plan and a date for what happens to the lot: sale, side-lot transfer to the neighbor, or land bank.
  • Lien and pursue cost recovery on every privately owned building the city has to take down.
  • Get buildable city-owned lots back on the tax rolls — sold, transferred to the neighbor, or bundled through the land bank for new homes.
  • Lower lot size requirements to fit the lots and buildings we already have.
  • Cut unnecessary parking requirements that eat up land and kill small projects before they start.
  • Bring hybrid zoning citywide. Propose to City Council the same kind of code downtown got in 2020, so mixed-use and multi-unit buildings like the ones that built our neighborhoods can go up again.

03

Blight is public safety

You cannot arrest your way out of an empty block.

The Elmira Police Department ranks with some of the best departments in the country.

The hand tied behind their back is a city that hands them block after block of vacant and burned-out structures and calls it a police matter when homeless break in. More than half the arrests our officers make are for low-level offenses, a majority being trespassing. You cannot arrest your way out of an empty block. A burned-out house next door doesn’t get solved by a patrol car — it gets solved by code enforcement, a lien, a demolition, and something built back on the lot.

What I’ll do

  • Treat code enforcement as a public safety function and staff it that way — the vacant structure is the crime scene nobody is assigned to.
  • Back the department in public, and help them get the job done correctly.

Zombie houses are dragging down our neighborhoods

Nearly 15% of Elmira homes are vacant — about 1 in 7, by the City’s own count.1 Every one of them lowers the value of the homes around it and invites break-ins, fires, and trouble.

The tools already exist. Since 2016, state law has required the banks foreclosing on abandoned homes to inspect, secure, and maintain them, with penalties of up to $500 a day, and it lets cities enforce it.2 Meanwhile, Elmira’s fee for failing to register a vacant building is $50.3 In 2024 the City announced a “Fight on Blight” with a zombie house task force, then repealed its public list of problem properties after five months and two lists.4

Let’s give code enforcement the tools they need.

What I’ll do

  • Staff and fund code enforcement to do the job.
  • Raise the cost of leaving a building unsecured.
  • Hold banks to the state zombie property law and collect the fines.
  • Get abandoned houses to the Land Bank faster, so they’re rebuilt, not just knocked down.
  • Help homeowners repair, not just write tickets.

Homelessness has outgrown the system built for it

In January 2019, the county counted an average of 68 homeless people on any given day in the Elmira area. By 2023, that number was 382. That is more than five times as many in four years.1 The number of people getting homeless services in Chemung County rose from 1,357 in 20232 to 1,638 in 2024, and 331 of them were children.3

Our neighborhoods feel that growth. There are encampments under the Clemens Center Parkway bridge and along the Lackawanna Trail. A fire at the bridge encampment closed part of it.4 People break into vacant houses for shelter, and in winter that means an open flame in a building with no working heat or safe wiring, a few feet from a family next door. Every call like that sends out a fire engine and a patrol car, and the city pays for both.

It costs taxpayers directly, too. In the first seven months of 2023 alone, more than $3 million went to putting homeless people up in motels. That money came from the city, the county, the state, and federal taxpayers.2

Many residents tell me a lot of these people aren’t from here, and that they come for the treatment clinics and the benefits. That may be true. But nobody publishes the numbers, and you can’t fix what you refuse to measure. Every Point-in-Time interview and every county intake already asks people where they last lived. Elmira taxpayers deserve to see that answer.

Compassion is not the same as enabling. Allowing homeless encampments to remain indefinitely, distributing food on the street without connecting people to services, and turning a blind eye to vacant properties being occupied only perpetuates the problem. We cannot continue policies that make it easier for people to remain on the streets than to accept the help available to them.

Elmira needs to draw a line. We have resources, shelters, and organizations ready to help those experiencing homelessness. We need to work with those organizations to get people off our streets and into the services available to them. But the days of looking the other way must come to an end. Public spaces are not permanent residences, vacant buildings are not shelters, and our neighborhoods should not have to live with encampments, fires, trespassing, and unsafe conditions. We will offer help, but we must also enforce the law. If someone refuses the assistance available to them, that does not give them the right to occupy public spaces, trespass on private property, or disregard the rules everyone else is expected to follow. We can be compassionate without surrendering control of our neighborhoods.

What I’ll do

  • Publish the numbers. Ask the County and Catholic Charities to release, every year, where people seeking homeless services last lived (by county and state), plus the police and fire calls and overtime tied to vacant buildings and encampments.
  • Close the buildings. When someone breaks into a vacant building, it gets secured and boarded fast, and the owner pays for it through a lien. Owners of vacant buildings must register, secure, and insure them.
  • A ticket home that actually lands. Work with the County on a reunification program: fare home for anyone who wants it, but only when someone on the other end has been verified to take them in, with a follow-up call to confirm they arrived.
  • No tolerated camps, anywhere. Council passed a camping ban in 2024, with fines of $350 to $500 and up to 90 days in jail.6 The Supreme Court has upheld laws like it.7 But camps keep coming back. I’ll enforce it the same way on every block, and pair every enforcement with a real offer of a shelter bed.
  • A standing trespass program. Owners of vacant buildings sign one authorization on file with EPD, so officers can remove and charge trespassers on the spot instead of tracking the owner down first.
  • Bring the help indoors.Work with local churches and community organizations to strengthen the resources available through our community kitchen and shelter. By bringing services under one roof, we can ensure people have access to meals, shelter, and caseworkers who can help them take the next steps toward stability and independence.
  • Every county houses its own. State guidance expects each county to help homeless residents within its own borders.9 I’ll press the County to hold other counties to that, so they don’t send their homeless to Elmira motels.
